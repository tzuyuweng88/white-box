Build an Interactive Web Prototype: White Box, Values-Guided Ethical Learning

1. Project Overview

Build a polished, fully interactive, desktop-first web prototype for an educational platform called White Box.

White Box helps K–12 students develop AI literacy and learn to use artificial intelligence thoughtfully and responsibly.

This prototype focuses on one feature: Values-Guided Ethical Learning.

Our primary persona is Sarah, a Grade 10 student.

The purpose is to help students:

1. Explore and understand ethical values.
2. Develop their own interpretations of these values.
3. Recognize how values relate to responsible AI use.
4. Practice ethical reasoning through realistic dilemmas.
5. Understand that different values can sometimes conflict.
6. Reflect on their decisions and reconsider their priorities.
7. Gradually build a personalized AI Values Guide.
8. Continue revising that guide throughout their learning journey.

Core design philosophy:

The Values Guide is a living artifact, not a one-time assignment.

Students should be able to develop, apply, question, and revise their understanding of ethical values over time.

The platform must encourage independent thinking rather than prescribe one universally correct ethical answer.

2. Technical Requirements

Build this as a working web application.

Preferred stack:

* React
* TypeScript
* Tailwind CSS
* Vite
* Lucide React icons
* LocalStorage for persistence

Use a clean component-based architecture.

The prototype does not require authentication, a database, or a backend.

All interactions should work with realistic local state.

Do not create static screenshots or placeholder interactions.

Every visible button should have a meaningful function.

Use responsive layouts, but prioritize a desktop viewport around 1440px wide.

Use accessible HTML controls, keyboard navigation, clear focus states, and sufficient color contrast.

The visual direction should feel:

10. Dilemma 1: The Group Project

Scenario

“You’re working on a group presentation with three classmates. Your presentation is tomorrow.

One teammate, Alex, has been having trouble writing their part in English. Alex has worked hard on it but feels nervous about presenting.

You know an AI tool that could help improve the writing. Your teacher allows AI for editing, but students must explain how they used it.

You want to help Alex and make sure your group does well. But you’re not sure whether you should suggest using AI.”

Question

What would you do next?

Options:

A. Ask Alex whether they’d like to use AI together to improve their section.

B. Use AI to improve Alex’s section, then show Alex the changes.

C. Keep Alex’s writing as it is.

D. Suggest that Alex use AI on their own.

E. I have another idea.

If E is selected, display a text field.

Do not immediately mark answers correct or incorrect.

After selecting an option, move to a values reflection screen.

11. Dilemma 2: A Convincing AI Answer

Scenario

“You’re writing an essay about how social media affects teenagers.

You ask an AI tool for information, and it gives you a convincing answer with several statistics.

One statistic would make your argument much stronger.

But when you look for the original study, you can’t find it.

Your essay is due soon, and you’ve already spent a lot of time working on it.”

Question

What would you do next?

Options:

A. Use the statistic because the AI answer sounds convincing.

B. Leave out the statistic until you can verify it.

C. Ask the AI to provide more sources and check whether those sources are real.

D. Rewrite the argument using information you’ve already verified.

E. I have another idea.

This scenario should explore tensions involving:

* Honesty
* Responsibility
* Independence
* Time pressure

Make clear through the learning experience that AI-generated information can be inaccurate, even when presented confidently.

Avoid implying that fabricated evidence becomes acceptable because a student personally values efficiency.

12. Dilemma 3: A Friend’s Photo

Scenario

“Your friend Maya posts a photo of herself online.

You think it would be fun to use an AI image tool to turn the photo into a cartoon.

You want to share the result in your group chat.

Maya has shared photos with your friends before, but you’ve never asked whether she’s comfortable with her pictures being uploaded to an AI tool.

The AI tool also asks you to upload the original photo.”

Question

What would you do next?

Options:

A. Upload the photo and share the cartoon because Maya posted the original publicly.

B. Ask Maya whether she’s comfortable with you using her photo.

C. Create the cartoon but don’t share it.

D. Choose a different image that doesn’t show a real person.

E. I have another idea.

This scenario should explore:

* Privacy
* Respect
* Responsibility
* Consent
* Potential consequences of sharing personal information

Do not treat posting a photo publicly as automatic permission for someone else to upload it to an AI service.

13. Shared Dilemma Interaction Flow

All three dilemmas must follow the same interactive structure.

Step A: Read the Scenario

Show the situation clearly.

Include:

* Scenario title
* Story
* Relevant illustration or icon
* Possible actions

Button: Continue

Step B: Choose an Action

Allow students to select a response.

They can also write their own idea.

Button: Explore My Choice

Step C: Identify Relevant Values

Title:

Which Values Matter Here?

Question:

“Which values do you think are important in this situation?”

Show all six values.

Students can select multiple values.

Then ask:

“Why do these values matter to you here?”

Provide an optional reflection field.

Button: Continue

Step D: Explore Value Tensions

Title:

When Values Pull in Different Directions

Display two relevant values side by side.

For example:

Respect:

“Taking other people’s feelings, ideas, and choices seriously.”

Fairness:

“Considering whether people have fair opportunities.”

Question:

“How might these values lead you to think about the situation differently?”

Allow a short written response.

Button: Continue

Step E: Explore Possible Consequences

Title:

What Might Happen Next?

Display:

* Possible benefits of the selected action
* Possible concerns
* Another person’s perspective
* A question to consider

These should be specifically written for each scenario and response.

Do not generate generic consequences.

Do not present every option as equally safe or acceptable.

Where relevant, clearly explain factual considerations such as privacy, consent, AI inaccuracies, and school rules.

Button: Think About My Decision

Allow students to return and explore another option.

Step F: Make or Reconsider the Decision

Ask:

“After thinking about the possibilities, what would you choose?”

Allow students to keep their original choice or select a different one.

Ask:

“What matters most in your decision?”

Provide a text field.

Button: Continue to My Values

20. Educational Design Principles

This application is intended for students, so wording must be carefully designed.

Follow these principles:

Use student-friendly language.

Avoid unexplained terms such as “ethical framework,” “algorithmic accountability,” or “value prioritization.”

Encourage independent judgment.

Avoid telling students which personal value they should prioritize.

Avoid moral scoring.

Do not use “Correct!” or “Wrong!” for nuanced ethical choices.

Recognize meaningful constraints.

Personal values do not override privacy, consent, safety, or school rules.

Support uncertainty.

Include opportunities to say “I’m not sure yet.”

Encourage perspective-taking.

Help students consider how their decisions might affect other people.

Respect student autonomy.

Students should decide whether to revise their personal guidelines.

Support ongoing learning.

The Values Guide should remain editable throughout the experience.

Use these Stanford resources as educational references:

Stanford CRAFT:
https://craft.stanford.edu/resource/what-principles-should-guide-ethical-use-of-ai/

Stanford HAI K–12 Education:
https://hai.stanford.edu/education/k-12

Use these resources for educational grounding, not as visual templates.

Do not claim that Stanford endorses White Box.
